# Adult and lifelong learning

S6 identifies an educational setting. It does not imply a university background, workplace goal, advanced subject knowledge or one preferred teaching method. This guide and its exercises are authored planning guidance, not observed learning outcomes.

## 0. Capability contract and prediction

The educator can distinguish professional experience from competence in the target subject, design suitable entry diagnostics, connect learning with the learner's stated purpose, and offer feasible support and assessment.

**Puzzle:** An experienced professional joins a beginner course in an unfamiliar subject and asks for a demonstration. Should the instructor withhold it because adults are expected to direct their own learning? Predict what evidence should determine the support.

A useful decision depends on the learner's target skills, current performance, purpose and requested support. Adult status is not evidence that an example is unnecessary. Ask, demonstrate when appropriate, and check what the learner can subsequently do.

## 1. Intake before selecting a method

The [UNESCO recommendation](../../60-evidence/sources/evidence-unesco-2015-adult-learning.md) includes adult learning beyond professional training. Use the learner's actual purpose, including personal interest, academic study, basic skills or community participation.

| Dimension | Gather | Use in planning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | What the learner wants to do or explore and why | Relevant goals and examples without inventing a workplace payoff |
| Prior knowledge | A suitable task, explanation or existing artifact | Entry point, prerequisite preparation and optional accelerated paths |
| Experience | Relevant experiences and possible conflicting assumptions | Connections to new content, with checks that the analogy fits |
| Time and access | Work/care responsibilities, language, materials, devices and accommodations | Feasible sessions, catch-up routes and independent work |
| Agency and support | Desired choices, preferred support and familiarity with learning tasks | Shared decisions and scaffolding that can change with evidence |

Do not infer cognitive decline, rigidity, motivation or self-direction from chronological age. Ask about actual barriers and observe performance on an appropriate task.

## 2. Worked course segment

**Authored example:** An adult beginner wants to compare the output rates of two craft activities. The data below are invented for the exercise, not observations of workers. Confirm division and the meaning of a rate before proceeding.

Outcome O1: Given item counts and elapsed hours, calculate items per hour with the unit. Outcome O2: Explain what must be comparable before interpreting a difference in rates.

| Phase | Task and reasoning | Evidence and feedback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Entry probe | Ask what information is needed to compare 12 items with 15 items | Check whether the learner asks for time and comparable items; do not score total count alone as rate understanding |
| Worked example | Activity A: 12 items in 3 hours gives 4 items/hour. B: 15 items in 5 hours gives 3 items/hour | Explain why the larger total need not be the larger rate |
| Guided practice | Compare 8 items in 2 hours with 9 items in 3 hours | Keys: 4 and 3 items/hour; ask the learner to explain the division and units |
| Independent attempt | Compare 12 items in 4 hours with 14 items in 7 hours | Keys: 3 and 2 items/hour; accept an equivalent valid explanation |
| Boundary check | Ask whether rates alone show quality or fairness of the comparison | Key: they do not; the items, time definitions and intended comparison need to be appropriate |
| Later transfer | Supply another small table and ask for a justified comparison | Record which support remains necessary and revisit a prerequisite if needed |

Proposed contact time: entry 4 + example 6 + guided practice 8 + independent attempt 5 + feedback 4 + reflection 3 = 30 minutes. A proposed later five-minute revisit is additional. These durations are planning choices, not biological attention limits.

Offer an accessible table or spoken presentation when the target is rate reasoning rather than reading print. Preserve any necessary accommodations during the independent attempt. A calculator's appropriateness depends on whether arithmetic execution or interpretation is being assessed.

## 3. Dialogue and error analysis

Learner: “B made more, so its rate is higher.” Educator: “How long did each activity take? What would each count be for one hour?” The learner's response helps distinguish attention to totals, confusion about division and uncertainty about units.

**Non-example:** “You are an experienced adult; discover the formula yourself.” This substitutes an age-based assumption for evidence of the target skill. Another non-example is recording a copied worked solution as proof of independent calculation.

## 4. Adaptation and failure recovery

| Observation | Check before interpreting it | Possible next move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Learner wants a worked example | Prior experience with this particular task | Demonstrate, then fade support based on a new attempt |
| Learner challenges the relevance | Their actual purpose and the course's stated scope | Adjust examples or scope; explain unavoidable prerequisites |
| Assignments are incomplete | Time, instructions, access, workload and competing responsibilities | Agree a feasible route and preserve the core learning goal |
| Practice succeeds but transfer fails | Changed conditions and availability of cues | Compare examples and recheck the relevant concept |

A reflective sequence of attempt, review, explanation and retry is one possible design. It is not a compulsory cycle for every adult or subject. Historical andragogy, experiential-learning and action-learning theories are research leads, not automatic evidence of superior retention. Earlier unsupported numerical benefits are not retained.

## 5. Hinge decision

A learner has substantial expertise in another field but is new to this course's content. What should determine the starting support?

A. Remove all examples because the learner is an adult. B. Use evidence from a suitable entry task and discuss the learner's goals and support needs. C. Assign advanced tasks solely from job seniority. D. Infer inability from the first error.

**Key: B.** A assumes universal self-direction; request task-specific evidence. C transfers status across domains without justification; check prerequisites. D overinterprets one response; use a follow-up and inspect conditions. Ask for the learner's reasoning rather than assigning a unique misconception from the selected option.

## 6. Practice and transfer for the educator

**Guided practice:** Adapt the rate segment for a learner whose division is uncertain. Keep the comparison goal but specify a prerequisite activity and how you will check it.

**Independent practice:** Design a short course segment for a personal-interest topic. Explain why its goal does not need a workplace benefit, identify subject sources, and map the goal to an assessment.

**Transfer:** Adapt the same segment for an experienced learner with limited time. Identify which evidence could justify skipping preparation and which assessment still needs to be completed.

## 7. Execution and evidence boundaries

Before teaching, confirm goals, prior knowledge and feasible workload. During teaching, collect evidence from attempts and explanations, provide actionable feedback and adjust support. After teaching, inspect an appropriate independent task and later application, recording context and limitations.

[UNESCO](../../60-evidence/sources/evidence-unesco-2015-adult-learning.md) supplies a broad normative frame; [CAST](../../60-evidence/sources/evidence-cast-2024-udl-guidelines.md) informs access options. The [IES practice summary](../../60-evidence/sources/evidence-ies-2007-organizing-instruction.md) may inform practice choices within its stated school/postsecondary scope; extension to another adult setting remains an authored adaptation. None proves an effect for this example. Expert review and learner evaluation remain separate tasks.
