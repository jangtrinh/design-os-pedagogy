# Harvard Empirical Case: Eric Mazur (Harvard University, 1990–2001)

![Peer Instruction ConcepTest](../../assets/peer_instruction_conceptest_1789439965233.jpg)

## 1. Historical Context & The Harvard Epistemic Crisis
* **Key Figure**: Prof. Eric Mazur, Balkanski Professor of Physics and Applied Physics at Harvard University.
* **Initial Situation (Fall 1990)**:
  * Mazur was an acclaimed instructor with stellar student evaluations (4.5/5.0).
  * He was confident his polished theatrical lectures and experimental demonstrations imparted deep mastery of introductory Newtonian mechanics (Physics 11).
* **The Empirical Shock**:
  * Mazur administered David Hestenes’ *Force Concept Inventory (FCI)* — a diagnostic test measuring Newtonian physical intuition without computational math.
  * Mazur assumed Harvard undergraduates would find the test trivially easy.
  * **Result: Harvard students failed catastrophically.** They could calculate complex derivatives on routine exam questions but possessed the pre-Newtonian misconceptions of ancient Aristotle regarding force and momentum.

---

## 2. The Peer Instruction Intervention
Mazur abandoned traditional lecturing, replacing it with the **ConcepTest Cycle**:
1. **Targeted Micro-Lecture (7–10 minutes)**: Introducing one core physical concept.
2. **ConcepTest Hinge Question (1 minute)**: A diagnostic multiple-choice conceptual item isolating a common misconception.
3. **Individual Silent Polling (1 minute)**: Students commit individually via clickers.
4. **Peer Discussion (2–3 minutes)**: If 30%–70% answer correctly, students are instructed: *"Find a neighbor who chose a different answer and convince them."*
5. **Revote & Class Explanation (2 minutes)**: Reteaching only if persistent confusion remains.

---

## 3. Quantitative 10-Year Harvard Longitudinal Data (Crouch & Mazur, 2001)
* **Sample**: $N > 3,000$ Harvard premed and engineering undergraduates over 10 years.
* **Normalized Learning Gain**:
  $$ g = \frac{\text{Post-test} - \text{Pre-test}}{100 - \text{Pre-test}} $$
  * Traditional Lecture baseline (Mazur 1985–1990): **$g = 0.23$** (Typical across didactic physics courses).
  * Peer Instruction (Mazur 1991–2000): **$g = 0.74$** (A 3x increase in conceptual learning efficiency).
* **Standard Problem Solving**: Traditional quantitative examination scores did *not* drop; they increased by $+0.35$ SD because students actually understood what the equations represented.

---

## 4. Key Takeaways for Pedagogical Engineering
* Active peer debate forces students to externalize mental models.
* A classmate who just grasped the concept is often more pedagogically effective than the expert professor, because the peer still understands the novice bottleneck.
