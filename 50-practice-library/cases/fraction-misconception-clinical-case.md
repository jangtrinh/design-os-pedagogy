## 1. Case Intake & Diagnostic Artifacts

* **Learner**: Kevin (Age 11, Grade 6).
* **Academic Baseline**: Punctual, respectful, completes all homework on time. Scored in the 55th percentile on 5th-grade state exams.
* **Teacher Observation**: Kevin’s math teacher, Mr. Davies, notices a bizarre pattern in Kevin’s latest quiz:

```
QUIZ RESULTS:
Problem 1: Calculate 1/5 + 2/5 = 3/5          ──► CORRECT (100%)
Problem 2: Calculate 4/7 - 1/7 = 3/7          ──► CORRECT (100%)

Problem 3: Which fraction is larger: 1/8 or 1/5?
   Kevin's Answer: 1/8 is larger.
   Kevin's Written Rationale: "Because 8 is bigger than 5, so 1/8 has to be bigger."

Problem 4: Place the fraction 3/4 on the number line below:
   [ 0 ]──────────────[ 1 ]──────────────[ 2 ]──────────────[ 3 ]──────────────[ 4 ]
   Kevin's Answer: Placed a dot directly on top of the number 3.
```

## 2. Decision Point 1: The Clinical Crossroad

You are reviewing Kevin's paper during a planning period. 

**What is your immediate pedagogical decision? (Commit to one option before reading further):**

* **Option A**: Give Kevin a rules worksheet with the rule: *"When numerators are identical, the smaller denominator is the bigger fraction"*, and have him memorize it through 20 practice drills.
* **Option B**: Teach Kevin the "Butterfly Trick" (cross-multiplying numerators and denominators) so he can calculate which fraction is larger mechanically without thinking.
* **Option C**: Conclude that Kevin has low mathematical aptitude and refer him to remedial special education.
* **Option D**: Recognize that Kevin has acquired procedural algorithmic rules without conceptual understanding of numerical magnitude; pivot immediately to a **1D Number Line and physical paper-folding intervention** to dismantle the Whole Number Bias.

---

### Diagnostic Distractor Analysis of Options A, B, C, D

| Option | Clinical Diagnosis | Pedagogical Consequence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Option A** | **The Procedural Band-Aid Fallacy** | Kevin will memorize the new verbal rule temporarily, but within two weeks, his working memory will mix it up with the regular comparison rule ($8 > 5$), causing renewed confusion. |
| **Option B** | **The Toxic Mnemonic Trap** | Cross-multiplication tricks actually **worsen the Whole Number Bias**! They teach students that fractions are pairs of whole numbers to be cross-multiplied ($1 \times 5 = 5$ vs $1 \times 8 = 8$), completely bypassing the concept of rational magnitude. |
| **Option C** | **The Deficit Attribution Fallacy** | Kevin's brain is executing a completely rational heuristic based on 5 years of natural number counting. The fault lies in curriculum delivery that detached algorithms from spatial representations. |
| **Option D** | **TARGET (The Master Clinical Move)** | **Number Line Representation**. Forces the learner to conceptualize fractions as single points on a continuous magnitude scale between 0 and 1, directly confronting the integer bias. |

## 3. Epistemic Root Cause: The Whole Number Bias (Ni & Zhou, 2005)

From infancy to age 10, human children spend 100% of their mathematical lives learning natural counting numbers:
$$1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8...$$
In this natural number system:
1. Every number has a discrete successor ($3$ comes right after $2$).
2. Larger symbols always denote larger magnitudes ($8 > 5$).
3. Multiplication always makes a number bigger ($4 \times 3 = 12$).
4. Division always makes a number smaller ($12 \div 3 = 4$).

When learners encounter **Rational Numbers (Fractions)**, every single one of these automated schemas is shattered:
* There is no discrete successor (between $\frac{1}{4}$ and $\frac{1}{2}$ lie infinite fractions).
* Larger numerical symbols can denote smaller magnitudes ($\frac{1}{8} < \frac{1}{5}$).
* Multiplying by a proper fraction makes numbers smaller ($12 \times \frac{1}{2} = 6$).
* Dividing by a proper fraction makes numbers bigger ($12 \div \frac{1}{2} = 24$).

Because Kevin’s brain was never guided through this paradigm shift, his working memory uses a survival heuristic: **he treats the numerator and denominator as two independent whole numbers glued together by a line**.

## 4. The 3-Phase Clinical Remediation Protocol (WWC Guidelines)

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ PHASE 1: CONCRETE PAPER FOLDING (The Disconfirming Anomaly) │
│ - Take two identical strips of paper.                       │
│ - Fold Strip 1 into 2 parts (halves).                       │
│ - Fold Strip 2 into 8 parts (eighths).                      │
│ - Compare the physical sizes. Ask: "Which piece would you   │
│   rather have if this were a chocolate bar?"                │
└─────────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────┘
                              ▼
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ PHASE 2: THE NUMBER LINE AS MAGNITUDE (The Spatial Bridge)  │
│ - Draw a large 1-meter number line from 0 to 1 on the desk. │
│ - Place benchmarks: 0, 1/2, and 1.                          │
│ - Physically walk or place markers for 1/8 and 1/5.         │
│ - Anchor rule: "Fractions are a SINGLE location in space."  │
└─────────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────┘
                              ▼
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ PHASE 3: ABSTRACT CONSOLIDATION & ERROR CRITIQUE            │
│ - Present student work containing the Whole Number Bias.    │
│ - Learner writes an explanation diagnosing the peer's error.│
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## 5. Annotated Live Dialogue Transcript: Socratic Cognitive Conflict

* **Teacher**: *"Kevin, I have two identical candy strips here. I am going to share Strip A with you and 4 friends—so 5 people total. I am going to share Strip B with 8 people. In which group do you get a bigger piece to eat?"*
* **Kevin (Hesitates, then smiles)**: *"The group with 5 people! If there are 8 people, you have to cut it into tiny little slivers!"*
* **Teacher**: *"Look at your paper from yesterday. You wrote that $\frac{1}{8}$ is bigger than $\frac{1}{5}$. What does the 8 in the denominator actually mean?"*
* **Kevin (The Epiphany)**: *"The 8 means how many people are sharing... so more people means smaller pieces!"*
* **Teacher (Cementing the spatial schema)**: *"Look at our number line on the wall from 0 to 1. Here is $\frac{1}{2}$. Where does $\frac{1}{5}$ live? Where does $\frac{1}{8}$ live? Which one is closer to zero?"*
* **Kevin**: *"$\frac{1}{8}$ is closer to zero because it’s smaller. $\frac{1}{5}$ is further to the right!"*
* **Teacher**: *"Look at Problem 4 on your quiz: where did you put $\frac{3}{4}$?"*
* **Kevin (Laughs in embarrassment)**: *"I put it at 3! I thought the 3 meant the whole number 3. But $\frac{3}{4}$ is less than 1 whole! It has to be right here, three-quarters of the way between 0 and 1!"*

## 6. Post-Intervention Transfer Check

To verify schema acquisition (avoiding the illusion of competence), administer an isomorphic transfer item:

> *"Which fraction is larger: $\frac{7}{8}$ or $\frac{8}{9}$? Explain without cross-multiplying."*
* **Novice Response (Whole Number Bias)**: *"$\frac{8}{9}$ because 8 and 9 are bigger numbers."* (Flawed reasoning, accidental correct answer).
* **Master Conceptual Response**: *"Both fractions are missing exactly one piece to make a whole ($1 - \frac{1}{8} = \frac{7}{8}$ and $1 - \frac{1}{9} = \frac{8}{9}$). Since ninths are smaller than eighths, $\frac{8}{9}$ is missing a smaller piece than $\frac{7}{8}$, which means $\frac{8}{9}$ is closer to 1 and therefore larger."*

## 7. Empirical Evidence & Longitudinal Impact

| Study | Methodology | Impact |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Siegler et al. (2012) Psychological Science** | Longitudinal analysis of UK and US cohorts ($N > 4,000$) | **Elementary fraction knowledge uniquely predicts high school algebra performance and overall graduation rates**, even after controlling for IQ, reading, and whole-number arithmetic. |
| **What Works Clearinghouse (WWC, 2010)** | Practice Guide on Fractions Instruction | Using the **Number Line as a central organizing representation** yielded significant, durable gains ($d = 0.52$) in conceptual fraction comparison and algebraic transfer. |
| **Ni & Zhou (2005)** | Systematic review of rational number development | Direct procedural instruction without conceptual grounding cemented the Whole Number Bias into high school; spatial-proportional modeling was required for conceptual restructuring. |
